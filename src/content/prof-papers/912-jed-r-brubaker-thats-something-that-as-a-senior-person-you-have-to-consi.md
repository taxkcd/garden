## Paper overview

This study explores how older adults think about and prefer to manage their digital data after they die. Through interviews with 16 older adults who frequently use digital devices, the research uncovers their awareness, preferences, and concerns about digital legacy, including who should manage their data, how long it should be kept, and the risks involved. The study highlights the importance of including older adults in designing systems for managing digital legacies and identifies opportunities like data donation and challenges such as technological obsolescence.

### Why it matters

**Research problem:** There is limited understanding of older adults’ perspectives on managing digital data after death, despite their proximity to end-of-life planning and increasing digital engagement. Existing research often overlooks this demographic’s specific preferences and challenges related to digital legacy and post-mortem data management.

**Why it matters:** As digital footprints increasingly outlive individuals, managing digital legacy raises important privacy, ethical, and emotional issues. Older adults are a key group likely to engage in end-of-life planning but are underrepresented in research, which limits the design of effective, user-centered digital legacy systems that address their unique needs and concerns.

**Key contributions:**

- Provides an empirical understanding of older adults’ preferences and motivations for end-of-life data management.
- Identifies four key factors shaping these preferences: prior experience with death, technological changes, desire for positive remembrance, and concerns about risks.
- Highlights opportunities such as data donation for public good and challenges like technological obsolescence and changing social norms.
- Advocates for purposeful inclusion of older adults in digital legacy research and design.
