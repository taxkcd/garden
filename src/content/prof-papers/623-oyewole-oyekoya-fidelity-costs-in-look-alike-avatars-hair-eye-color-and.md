## Paper overview

This study investigates how changes in hair and eye color of digital avatars that look like real people affect how observers perceive them in mixed reality. It finds that changing eye color has a bigger negative impact on acceptance and humanlikeness than changing hair color, and that these effects add up without compounding. Familiarity with the person lowers acceptance overall but does not increase the penalty for deviations. The findings help guide avatar design for realistic virtual representations.

### Why it matters

**Research problem:** Users often alter their avatars away from their actual appearance, but the cost of these deviations on observers who know the person depicted has rarely been measured, especially for surface features like hair and eye color in mixed reality settings.

**Why it matters:** Look-alike avatars are increasingly used in collaborative mixed reality, where observers' judgments govern interaction outcomes. Understanding how deviations affect acceptance and perception is crucial for designing effective avatars that maintain social credibility and comfort.

**Key contributions:**

- Demonstrated that hair and eye color deviations independently reduce acceptance and humanlikeness and increase eeriness of look-alike avatars.
- Showed that the effects of hair and eye deviations combine additively without compounding.
- Found that eye color deviation carries roughly twice the perceptual cost of hair color deviation on acceptance.
- Established that familiarity with the depicted person lowers acceptance and humanlikeness but does not increase the penalty for deviations.
- Provided empirical evidence focusing on observer perception rather than self-avatar perception in mixed reality.
