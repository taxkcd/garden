## Paper overview

This paper studies the challenges faced by formerly incarcerated individuals (returning citizens) in searching for jobs online, focusing on their digital literacy and experiences with discrimination. Through workshops and focus groups with returning citizens and their social support networks, the authors identify specific difficulties and propose design recommendations for digital job search tools that better support this community, emphasizing human assistance, clear job descriptions, and alternative application methods like video.

### Why it matters

**Research problem:** Returning citizens face significant barriers in online job search due to criminal records, employment gaps, limited digital literacy, and discrimination, especially racial bias. Existing digital tools do not adequately address their unique needs or the intersection of digital literacy and reentry challenges.

**Why it matters:** Over 400,000 individuals return from incarceration annually in the U.S., with job search being critical for successful reintegration. Digital literacy is increasingly necessary for job search, yet returning citizens often lack these skills and face compounded discrimination, limiting their employment opportunities and community well-being.

**Key contributions:**

- Empirical insights into how returning citizens navigate digital job search challenges and disclosure of felony status.
- Identification of specific digital tool features desired by returning citizens, such as clear job descriptions, auto-fill for forms, video tutorials, and human assistance options.
- An updated version of the Digital Rehabilitation Framework emphasizing social, personal, and cultural factors impacting digital reentry and employment.
- Focus on Black returning citizens’ experiences with racial discrimination and job search strategies.
- Design recommendations co-created with returning citizens and their social support networks.

## About the professor

**Tawanna Dillahunt** — Research Investigator, School of Information, Academic Program Director, Master of Science in Information, School of Information, University of Michigan.

Research interests: My research focuses on the intersection of technology, employment, and community well-being. At the forefront of my earlier research is the development of digital tools tailored to the unique needs of communities facing economic challenges, primarily in Southeastern Michigan. My work also focuses on how digital technologies can benefit these communities by providing equitable transportation, support for migrant adaptation, access to healthful food and telemedicine, and remote work capabilities. Specifically, my team and I have developed digital employment tools that address the needs of job seekers with limited digital literacy and education; assessed real-time ridesharing and online grocery delivery applications among lower-income and transportation-scarce groups; and proposed models for novice entrepreneurs to build their technical capacity. Today, we are expanding our focus towards community-centric approaches, examining mentorship and digital literacy programs that empower economically disadvantaged communities.
