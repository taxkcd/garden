# 204 · Unprecedented female mutation bias in the aye-aye, a highly unusual lemur from Madagascar

## At a glance

- **Professor:** Matthew W. Hahn
- **Institution:** Indiana University
- **Paper:** [Unprecedented female mutation bias in the aye-aye, a highly unusual lemur from Madagascar](https://hahnlab.github.io/Publications/Wang_etal2025.pdf)
- **Authors:** Richard J. Wang, Yadira Peña-García, Muthuswamy Raveendran, R. Alan Harris, Thuy-Trang Nguyen, Marie-Claude Gingras, Yifan Wu, Lesette Perez, Anne D. Yoder, Joe H. Simmons, Jeffrey Rogers, Matthew W. Hahn
- **Year:** 2025

## Paper overview

This study reveals that unlike all other mammals studied so far, the aye-aye lemur shows a unique pattern where older mothers transmit more genetic mutations to their offspring than older fathers. This challenges the long-held belief that males contribute more mutations due to ongoing sperm cell divisions. The research also identifies candidate genes that might explain this unusual female mutation bias.

### Why it matters

**Research problem:** Understanding the causes and variability of sex-biased mutation rates in mammals, particularly the near-universal male mutation bias where males transmit more de novo mutations than females.

**Why it matters:** Male mutation bias has been a consistent pattern across mammals for over 75 years, but its underlying causes remain debated. Discovering exceptions to this pattern can provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of mutation and evolution, with implications for genetics, evolutionary biology, and disease.

**Key contributions:**

- Discovery of a strong maternal age effect on mutation rates in aye-ayes, contrasting with the typical paternal age effect in mammals.
- Identification that older aye-aye mothers transmit more mutations than older fathers, an unprecedented female mutation bias.
- Validation of mutation calls and phasing through computational and experimental methods including Sanger sequencing.
- Comparative analysis showing that baboons, rhesus macaques, and humans exhibit the expected male-biased mutation patterns.
- Phylogenetic analysis revealing that long-term substitution patterns in strepsirrhines still show male bias, suggesting recent or context-dependent female bias in aye-ayes.
